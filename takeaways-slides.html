European Power · Takeaways
The big idea

The plants that set Europe’s electricity price can no longer earn a good living from doing it.

Where Europe’s gas arrives from

When there is a lot of sun and wind at once, solar and wind farms sell into cheap hours and undercut each other. At the same time, a wave of new gas-export terminals* is locking in cheaper gas for years ahead.

Gas shipped from the US and Qatar still matters a lot: gas-fired plants are often the last ones needed to keep the lights on, so the gas price still sets the electricity price in many hours.

In short: more renewables and cheaper gas are both pushing the same electricity price lower. The next slides show how — and what that means for people who put money into power plants.
*LNG terminals: places that ship natural gas in liquid form across the ocean.

Cheaper gas ahead

Today’s gas spikes are temporary. Markets already expect gas near €20 later this decade.

What buyers lock in today for gas in the years ahead*
Where US gas exports went in 2025
How much gas Europe imported by ship

Short-term scares (a blocked shipping lane, a cold winter) can still push today’s price up. Looking further out, the market expects much lower prices — because a huge amount of new US export capacity has already been approved and is being built. Europe was the main destination for those US cargoes in 2025.

In short: by 2030, European gas is expected to settle near the cost of producing and shipping US gas (€15 to €22 per MWh†) — not near today’s crisis levels.
*The blue line is the main European gas benchmark (TTF): prices agreed today for delivery later. †MWh = megawatt-hour, a standard unit of energy. The shaded band is a rough cost floor for US gas delivered to Europe.
